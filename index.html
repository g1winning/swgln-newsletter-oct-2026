Fruit fly

Not this season: four posters for the fruit fly

Queensland fruit fly is now established in the Seymour district, spreading from backyards rather than orchards. Adults overwinter in sheltered trees; maggots develop in fruit left on the ground. A single unpicked tree in October can seed an entire street by January. Pick all your fruit, bag or boil what you won't eat, net what you want to keep, and set a bottle trap in August so you see them before they reach your apricots.

These four posters deliver the same message in four different ways. They are the network's own, free to print, ideal for shop windows, hall noticeboards, school fences, produce stalls and roadside stands. Each is A3, high-quality for printing. Download your favourite below, or ask for the full set with editable files. Let us know where you display them.

Lyndale Park: walk the farm, record the bush

Do you know Lyndale Park? Located on Tallarook-Pyalong Road, it spans 214 hectares with 2.6 kilometres of Sunday Creek frontage. Thirty households own and run it as a single farm. Since 2024, it has served as the network's demonstration site for the Climate Smart Agriculture Program, funded by the Goulburn Broken Catchment Management Authority through the Natural Heritage Trust. The program continues until 2028. In two years, the owners have fenced 19 hectares to exclude grazing, fenced stock off the creek, limed 11 hectares, sown multispecies fodder, and supplied water to troughs so dams can rest. They have begun turning a degraded dam into a wetland.

On Saturday 14 November, the property opens for the network's field day. Ecologist Peter Mitchell will lead a walk from one end of the farm through grey box and white box country, past the retired paddocks, new fencing and wetland. Along the way, the group will establish the farm's permanent photo points and enter the first vegetation condition records in Covram. Anyone with a phone can participate, and the method goes home with you. Start time and meeting point come with your booking; lunch is provided. Bring boots, a hat, water and a charged phone. All are welcome. Book with me on 0409 515 630 or swglandcare@gmail.com so we can cater for everyone.

Covram is the Condition Of Vegetation Rapid Assessment Method, a free phone app built by Ian Davidson of Covram Pty Ltd in Greensborough, following twenty years running Regeneration Solutions and time at Greening Australia as a wildlife biologist. His son Michael developed the software. Beneath it sits VAST (Vegetation Assets, States and Transitions), the framework published by Thackway and Lesslie in 2006 for describing how far a patch of bush has shifted from its natural state and its direction of change.

The process in the paddock is straightforward. Stand at the photo point, take a photograph, and answer a brief set of questions about what you see: habitat type, structural intactness, presence of large hollow-bearing trees, woody weeds, ground layer weediness, and native diversity. Covram stores the photograph, location and answers together with the date. Next year, return to the same spot, repeat the process, and compare results. The rating is made by you, using the VAST classes; the app's job is to maintain the record, enabling comparisons in five years, no matter who's taking part. It's a rapid method, not a comprehensive flora survey, and doesn't claim to be.

Funders now expect monitoring, and this is a form of monitoring any landholder can do without a consultant.

Why does it matter? A grant application that says, "We fenced the creek in 2024 and here are the dated photographs and condition records from three points since," is received differently from one that simply says, "We have done a lot of work." Funders now expect monitoring, and this is a form of monitoring any landholder can do without a consultant. The same record stays with the property, useful for succession conversations, a sale, a covenant, or planning for the next owner. Lyndale begins its series at the field day, and any member can start on their own property the same afternoon.

Before you come, install Covram and register with your name and email. For iPhone: apps.apple.com/au/app/covram-vegetation-monitoring/id6463190446. For Android: play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.covram.covram. The developer's site, with explanations and a short tutorial, is covram.com.au. You'll be invited to join the Lyndale project before the day so your first records go straight onto your own phone.

On the website

One hundred and twenty-six documents, one page

The Library page on the network's website now holds 126 documents: plans and reports about this district from Mitchell Shire, Strathbogie Shire, the Goulburn Broken CMA, Agriculture Victoria, various departments and researchers. These include biodiversity strategies, roadside management plans, weed guides, waterway studies, the catchment strategy, fire and flood plans, dam and pasture guides. Many were published, filed on council websites, then forgotten. Several are no longer available on the publisher's site. Now, they're all here, each with a two-sentence summary, the full abstract one click away, the PDF ready to open, and a link to the original if it still exists.

Search by keyword, place or topic. Ten topic buttons sort everything (weeds, waterways, fire, biodiversity, soil, planning and more); a publisher filter narrows results to one source. Type 'Kilmore Creek,' 'serrated tussock,' or 'Tallarook' and the page shows what's been written about it. For anyone preparing a grant application, submission to council or management plan for their property, the information is now an evening's reading, not a month's hunt.

The page is free, with no login required. Find it in the top bar at swglandcare.org.au, or go straight to swglandcare.org.au/library. More documents are added as they're found; if you have a report about this district that isn't there, email it to the network address and it'll join the shelf.

On Taungurung Country

On Taungurung Country: a day at Reedy Lake

On Tuesday 29 September, I spent the day on Taungurung Country. Biolinks Alliance organised the wawa biik cultural tour for partners in its Nature on Farms and Goldfields Forest Restoration projects, and the network was invited. We met at the Nagambie Lakes Regatta Centre, boarded the wawa biik bus, and travelled to Reedy Lake. Taungurung guides Ben and Anne welcomed us with a smoking ceremony, then led us through the site for the rest of the morning.

The lake was full after a wet winter. River red gums stood knee-deep in water, the forest floor was thick with wildflowers, and the grass trees on the rises have grown there for centuries, through countless fires. Ben and Anne shared the story of the lake, the people who have cared for it for thousands of years, and how it was managed before fences: fire, harvest, and seasons read from plants instead of calendars. Lunch was Indigenous teas and bush foods.

Biolinks Alliance

Biolinks Alliance made the day possible. Based in central Victoria, it connects nineteen Landcare networks, conservation management networks and environment groups, from the Grampians to the Alps. It runs pilot projects the rest of us can learn from: Nature on Farms, which brings natural solutions to working farms facing a drier climate; Goldfields Forest Restoration, reviving the box-ironbark forests; and Glideways, reconnecting glider habitat across the region. South West Goulburn has been a member for years, with Peter Mitchell as our representative for most of them.

biolinksalliance.org.au

wawa biik is Taungurung Cultural Experiences, operated from the Taungurung Land and Waters Council's office at 37 High Street, Broadford. Anyone can book a tour. The relationship the network has been building with the Council is now open: they see Landcare's work as closely aligned with their own, so where cultural waterway overlays cross the creeks we plant, their support will help shape our projects. The site of the former High Camp Railway Station which served Glenaroua families from 1890 until its closure in 1968 is being transformed into a picnic reserve.

The Friends of the Bendigo-Kilmore Rail Trail (FBKRT) were last year awarded a $38,252 Tiny Towns Fund grant from Regional Development Victoria (RDV), to support the revitalisation of the site in Crawfords Rd, High Camp.

Support funding of $6,500 is being provided by Mitchell Shire Council, along with $14,880 of 'in-kind' from a number of community groups.

Volunteers have spent the last few months clearing years of overgrowth and fallen tree branches at the neglected site adjacent to the High Camp Flora Reserve.

A new High Camp railway sign has been placed on the old platform, along with an information board, picnic table and bicycle rack. The site will be planted out in April with native trees and shrubs.

As part of the project volunteers have compiled a history of the Heathcote Junction to Heathcote line which, along with passengers, transported Glenaroua's dairy, livestock, timber and rabbit skins down south, returning on the journey north with household goods, superphosphate and the mail.

Several of Glenaroua's farming families were interviewed about their memories of the vital role the line once played in the district.

The history can be read at bendigokilmorerailtrail.com/high-camp

Disaster recovery

After the next disaster: what should happen to the debris?

Every disaster leaves a pile. After the January fires, it was burnt trees pushed up and burnt again or trucked away; after a flood, it's the timber and silt a creek dumps across a paddock; after a storm, it's fallen limbs. Councils and the agencies behind them clear it to a deadline, under contract, with the material treated as rubbish, because no one told them in advance it was anything else.

Landcare has learnt the hard way to ask early. Formal guidance for councils, agreed before disasters strike, works better than scrounging for material afterwards. The Goulburn Broken's Landcare coordinators and facilitators met at Euroa on 14 October and agreed to put such a list to the agencies responsible for recovery. Hollow logs should stay where they fall: they're the only hollows the paddock will have for fifty years. Sound trunks should go to a portable mill for the fences lost in the fire, not to the heap. Limbs can become stakes and guards for the replanting that follows. Firewood is what's left, not the first use. Who holds the machinery, what sizes are suitable, and what a volunteer crew is allowed to do are details to settle now.

Victorian Serrated Tussock Working Party

Cut the heads before they turn gold

250,000ha
of Victoria under serrated tussock, and more
20km
how far the wind carries the seed

Serrated tussock is a Weed of National Significance and infests more than 250,000 hectares of Victoria. One plant produces tens of thousands of seeds, which the wind can carry up to twenty kilometres. The seeds remain viable in the soil for seven years. Livestock will not eat it; at ninety percent fibre, it's nearly indigestible. The Working Party's March map shows new infestations following the main roads out of Melbourne, with Redesdale and Mia Mia named on our Heathcote side.

Spring is when it becomes visible. From late October, the flowering heads give the paddock a purple hue; eight to ten weeks later, the seed is ripe and the heads turn gold. Purple means you're in time. Gold means the seed is wind-borne, then it's your neighbour's problem too.

The job for the next month is straightforward. Walk the boundaries, then the roadsides. Pull the leaf between finger and thumb: serrated tussock rolls smoothly like a needle, while native tussocks feel flat-edged. Cut every flowering head, bag it, and mark the plant. Don't slash, mow or harvest through a patch in seed. Don't allow a contractor's machine on the property unless it's clean. Kill the plant itself in autumn, when herbicide windows open.

Purple means you're in time. Gold means the seed is wind-borne, then it's your neighbour's problem too.

Weeds don't respect property boundaries. A single plant left to seed this spring can become a paddock full in three years, so the neighbour checking over the fence in late October is doing you a favour. The Working Party's identification guides, treatment videos and control calendar are all free at serratedtussock.com. Ivan Carter answers calls from landholders who think they've found it on 0422 605 953 or info@serratedtussock.com. Mitchell Shire lists serrated tussock as one of its two featured weeds this spring, and its land management officers can help landholders write a weed plan before enforcement is ever an issue.

Victorian Landcare Forum

Two days at Pakenham

The Victorian Landcare Forum filled the Cardinia Cultural Centre at Pakenham for two days at the start of September, bringing together more than 220 facilitators, committee members and landholders from every catchment in the state. The conversations in the car park were as lively as those in the sessions. I attended on behalf of the network.

The session that filled fastest was on deer: Jillian Staton on how her Cape Liptrap neighbours in South Gippsland organised their own deer control, and Johannes Wenzel on why every catchment in the state is in the same position. What that means for us is in the deer article below.

The conversations in the car park were as lively as those in the sessions.

Carolyn Hall of the Mulloon Institute gave a talk that had people still discussing it the next day. "From catchment to coast: partnering with Landcare to rehydrate landscapes" was about slowing water as it moves through a catchment so it sinks in and stays, using structures in waterways and changing grazing beside them. The Institute's own creek near Braidwood is the proof. Victorian examples are fewer and more recent. New South Wales has an approval pathway for these works; Victoria does not, so whether a leaky weir is permitted as a waterway project or classified as a dam depends on the authority. Anyone with a creek on their property will understand why this matters.

In between, Anthony Gallacher launched Landcare Victoria's "Landscape Action Plan Handbook". The trade stands were full of tree guards and stakes (three of those suppliers have since quoted for the Argyle planting), and the Wednesday field trips headed into the Cardinia hills.

Pest animals

Deer: yours to shoot, no one's to manage

Deer have no such declaration. Every other mainland state has made one.

Start with what helps. Every deer in Victoria is classified as wildlife, and six species are considered game, but a separate order declares deer unprotected on private property. You may destroy deer damaging your property, crops or livestock at any time of year, with no game licence and no permit. All that's required is a firearms licence. Spotlights are allowed on your own land, anyone acting for you just needs your written permission, and you keep the meat. Hog deer are the exception.

So the practical reality is better than most landholders assume. The legal situation, however, is odd. Rabbits and foxes are declared pests, obliging every landholder to control them and providing a funding stream for the task. Deer have no such declaration. Every other mainland state has made one. Victoria has not, so there's no duty for landholders, no basis for coordinated action across boundaries, and no funding. Peter Jacobs of the Victorian Deer Control Community Network put it plainly: landholders receive no government support, funding or advice for control.

The cost is only counted where there's a receipt. Feral deer cost Australia $91.3 million in 2021, $69.1 million of that to agriculture. One projection put the cost to Victoria alone at $1.5 to $2.2 billion over thirty years if numbers aren't brought down. Neither figure includes an understorey browsed out over twenty years, a pugged creek crossing or a failed direct-seeding line. The authors of the Victorian projection said the biodiversity cost would likely be enormous and couldn't be priced. What's left out is the very part Landcare exists to protect.

In October 2025, the Victorian Government responded to the independent review of the Wildlife Act. The review recommended declaring all deer a pest animal. The government marked the recommendation "not supported", largely due to hunting interests: the Australian Deer Association argued that losing game status would cost 50,000 recreational hunters their access to public land and the Game Management Authority most of its revenue. That argument prevailed. The Outdoor Recreation Victoria Bill 2026, passed this year, merges the authority into a new body and leaves the status of deer unchanged.

The frustration is statewide. At the Landcare Forum in Pakenham, the deer session attracted the largest crowd, and Johannes Wenzel of the Victorian Deer Control Community Network described the same trend across every catchment: numbers rising, landholders left to manage deer alone. The one group that has found a way forward is at Cape Liptrap in South Gippsland, where Jillian Staton's neighbours formed the Cape Liptrap Community Deer Control Group. Every landholder signs a permission form, a map shows whose gates are open, commercial harvesters are paid, and a communications plan is published. None of this required a change of law. Nearly all of it could be adapted to Sunday Creek or Dabyminga, and the South Gippsland network has offered to share their paperwork. The one line they don't cross, and nor would we, is vetting or placing hunters on other people's land.

Our own representatives have said little. Annabelle Cleeland, Member for Euroa, has not raised deer in the Victorian Parliament. Sam Birrell, Member for Nicholls, has not raised them in Canberra. Of the five Northern Victoria members in the upper house, Gaelle Broad has come closest, asking the Minister for Agriculture on 17 June what more would be done about weeds and pests, deer among them, on Crown land. Senior Nationals argue in Parliament that deer are a serious pest and that more recreational hunting is the answer. Expect to hear that argument.

There is no inquiry, consultation or petition open, so a letter to your member is the available avenue. A letter won't change the law on its own, but it puts a named constituent, with an address in the electorate and a paddock of evidence, on the record against a position the member hasn't yet had to defend locally. Enough letters make the position harder to hold.

Write to Annabelle Cleeland, and to Gaelle Broad and Wendy Lovell in the upper house. For the National Feral Deer Action Plan, write to Sam Birrell. Keep it short and personal: where you farm, for how long, and what deer have done on your property: the trail camera count, lost seedlings, damaged fences, creek crossings. Ask for three things: declare deer an established pest animal, remove game status so control is not tied to hunting seasons, and fund control across property boundaries, because deer roam thousands of hectares and cannot be managed one paddock at a time. Ask for the member's position in writing. Send it before the election on Saturday 28 November. If enough members want to go further, an e-petition can be lodged with the Legislative Assembly and I will draft it.

Around the catchment

From our neighbours

The state awards: The Victorian Landcare Awards were presented at Fed Square on 15 October, with 99 nominations across 14 categories. TreeProject, the Melbourne volunteer nursery that grows the 4,000 seedlings Glenaroua plants next June, won the Environmental Volunteer Group award. Wodonga Urban Landcare Network was named Landcare Network of the Year. (Source: Landcare Australia's release, 15 October, landcareaustralia.org.au.)

Nearly $300,000 for the catchment: The Victorian Government Landcare Grants for 2026 have directed almost $300,000 to Goulburn Broken groups. Fire recovery work is funded at Goomalibee, Creightons Creek, Longwood East, the Euroa Arboretum and the Strathbogie Ranges Conservation Management Network. Sheep Pen Creek will study its lace monitors. (Source: Goulburn Broken CMA October news, gbcma.vic.gov.au.)

Longwood, nine months on: CMA staff returned in September to the vegetation transects set in April after the fire. Grasses and wildflowers are back in the ground layer, tree and shrub seedlings are coming up, and the insects, fungi, frogs and birds are returning. The severely burnt forest has a long road ahead. (Source: CMA's fire recovery page.)

Mitchell's mapping: The shire's own field mapping platform, M-SNAP, was a finalist in the Municipal Association of Victoria's innovation awards. At the Australian Plants Society expo in Wallan in September, more than 400 people attended and 27 households joined Gardens for Wildlife. (Source: Green Scene 16, 7 October, mitchellshire.vic.gov.au.)

Bird flu: The H5 strain is present in wild birds in Victoria. Leave sick or dead birds alone. Report five or more dead birds together, or any single dead waterbird, seabird, shorebird, raptor or marine mammal, to 1800 675 888. (Source: Landcare Victoria October newsletter.)

Platypus and dogs: October is Platypus Month. Off-leash dogs at the creek kill platypus; keep dogs on a lead near water and log any sighting at platypusspot.org. (Source: Waterwatch Victoria.)

The election: Victoria votes on Saturday 28 November. The Department of Energy, Environment and Climate Action enters caretaker mode from 3 November, so expect no new government decisions until December. Landcare Victoria has a two-page summary of what the sector is seeking in the next budget, useful for anyone talking to a candidate. landcarevictoria.org.au
